# Form, Not Fame, Makes Lullabies Soothe

The most-watched lullabies on YouTube are short cartoons of very old songs. Super Simple Songs' "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star" has **2.45 billion views at 2:34**, the Bengaluru studio Infobells' "Hush a Bye Baby" has **2.12 billion at 2:14**, and CoComelon's "Twinkle Twinkle" has **2.00 billion at 3:16**. In India the leaders are Infobells' Tamil thalattu "Chinna Chinna Paadhangal" (**1.69 billion**) and many uploads of "Chanda Mama Door Ke" (up to 788 million each), which is a 1955 Hindi film song and still under copyright. The calming power of these songs does not come from fame. Babies relax to lullabies they have never heard, even in a foreign language, because of the shape of the music: slow, simple, repetitive, narrow in pitch, soft, smooth, and falling at the end of each phrase. So an original Cheeko lullaby can calm a child as well as a famous one, and it earns the extra "familiar song" benefit simply by being played every night. Children usually fall asleep in about 20 minutes, so Cheeko should make 2.5 to 4 minute songs and play them inside a fixed 15 to 20 minute bedtime session that fades out and switches off, not an all-night loop. Lyrics should be about the moon, home, animals settling down and the child's name, with soft vowel endings, no threats, and nothing borrowed from film loris. The biggest safety levers sit in firmware, not in the music: a hard volume cap in sleep mode, an auto-off timer and a screen that goes dark. Cheeko can honestly promise "calming" and "a bedtime routine"; the research does not support "puts your child to sleep faster".

## Babies relax to the shape of a lullaby, even in a language they never heard

Scientists can now describe fairly precisely what makes a lullaby a lullaby. In a study of songs from 86 mostly small-scale societies, listeners who heard short excerpts identified lullabies and dance songs most accurately, and 1,000 raters **in the United States and India** judged the songs on tempo, beat and complexity ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29395919/); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/music-universal-language-8370/)). A larger 2019 analysis in Science found lullabies **less rhythmically complex** than other songs across world cultures ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7001657)). The Harvard Music Lab sums up the pattern: lullabies are typically **less accented, slower, smaller in pitch range and looser in beat** than other songs ([Bainbridge et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405)). Tempo alone does not define a lullaby. When lullabies and adult songs were matched for tempo, listeners still picked out the lullaby, using simplicity, repetition and **pitch that falls at the end of phrases** as cues. In the same research, Indian-descent mothers singing Hindi songs to their babies were recognised as baby-directed by both Indian and North American listeners ([Trehub 1993](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trehub.pdf)).

The key experiment for Cheeko is Bainbridge and colleagues' 2021 study. 144 US babies aged 2 to 14 months heard eight unfamiliar lullabies from other cultures and eight non-lullaby songs from the same cultures, each sung by one voice with no instruments. During the lullabies, **heart rate fell, pupils got smaller and skin arousal was damped**; during the other songs, heart rate did not change. The effect was the same at every age in the first year, and parents picked the lullaby as the song they would use to calm a fussy baby ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2020/10/research-shows-lullabies-in-any-language-relax-babies)). The study covered one Western country and short clips, babies paid attention for about 5 minutes, and it could not say which single feature does the work. Still, it is direct evidence that a baby does not need to know a song for it to calm them, which is exactly what an original lullaby needs.

Three more findings shape how a Cheeko lullaby should sound. First, **singing beats talking**. When parents tried to calm upset 8 and 10 month olds, expressive talking made distress worse, while both familiar and unfamiliar songs reduced it, and familiar songs worked best and drew more smiles ([UCSF Sound Health](https://soundhealth.ucsf.edu/node/8001)). Babies also stayed calm about twice as long to singing as to speech ([Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/singing-calms-baby-longer-than-talking)). Second, a lullaby does not need a high, chirpy voice. A 21-society study found that the raised pitch adults use with babies belongs to baby talk, not to song; songs for babies change tempo and tone colour instead ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2022/07/people-around-world-tend-to-talk-to-babies-in-higher-voice)). Third, **live beats recorded**. In a trial with 272 premature babies across 11 neonatal units, live lullabies lowered heart rate and helped feeding, and the authors warned that recorded music can overstimulate newborns because it cannot adapt to the baby ([Live Science](https://livescience.com/28710-music-therapy-premature-babies-health.html)). Motion matters too: being carried by a walking parent calmed crying babies within about 30 seconds, and the researchers suggest about 5 minutes of carrying, then 5 to 8 minutes of sitting, before laying a baby down ([RIKEN](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2022/20220914_1/index.html)).

Two popular beliefs do not hold up. No primary study establishes **60 to 80 beats per minute** as the ideal lullaby tempo; the figure appears only as background in a conference poster that itself calls tempo an open question ([TU Dresden](https://tu-dresden.de/ing/elektrotechnik/ias/socialtouch/ressourcen/dateien/Poster-IASAT25-Benjamin-2.pdf)). Field recordings of Thai lullabies sit at about 50 to 55 BPM ([J. Studies in Culture](https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JSC/article/view/278837)), while a commercial 2019 analysis found Spotify's lullabies averaged about 91 BPM in C major and 4/4 time ([Irish Post](https://www.irishpost.com/news/u2-song-named-among-top-10-tracks-to-send-kids-off-sleep-171249)), which suggests many commercial tracks run faster than traditional singing. The second belief is that recorded lullabies make children fall asleep faster. The best evidence is a small preschool nap study, 22 minutes with lullabies against 34 in silence, built on only 13 observations ([U. Oregon](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~rmoore/res-prenap.htm)), and a 2022 sleep-app study notes that the effect of music or stories on young children's sleep has not been reported ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9368592/)). The honest claim is that lullabies **calm** and support a routine, not that they cut the time to sleep.

## Familiar tunes, tired parents and YouTube's money rules drive the views

Four forces stack up behind the billion-view numbers. The first is biology, covered above: the form of a lullaby relaxes babies whatever the song. The second is **familiarity**. Nearly every top lullaby uses a public-domain tune such as Twinkle Twinkle, Rock-a-bye or Brahms' lullaby, which appears in almost every long-form hit ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=44tiZ7IP7zA)). Parents search for songs they already know, and demand attaches to the song rather than the channel: "Chanda Mama Door Ke" has several uploads with 390 to 788 million views each ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jFYiT3_65kE); [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FTgjhMQjEck)). Familiarity mainly buys discovery through search, which matters on YouTube but much less on a device already sitting in the bedroom.

The third force is plain **usefulness to tired parents**. In the US about 1 in 5 children use mobile devices to calm down, at mealtimes or to fall asleep, and 40% have their own tablet by age 2 ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight)). A parent presses play and leaves a 2 or 8-hour video running, which piles up watch time. The fourth is **YouTube's economics**. After Google and YouTube paid $170 million in 2019 to settle charges of targeting ads at children, videos marked "made for kids" have carried only non-personalised ads since January 2020, with forecasts of a 60 to 90% income loss for kids' creators ([Cartoon Brew](https://cartoonbrew.com/artist-rights/a-new-youtube-rule-is-threatening-animation-content-creators-heres-what-you-need-to-know-about-coppa-182883.html)). That pushes channels toward volume and very long, cheap videos; Fiverr sellers offer batches of lullaby sleep videos for $10 to $40 ([Fiverr](https://www.fiverr.com/creativepalash/lullaby-video-lullaby-music-baby-lullaby-lullabies-relaxing-sleeping-music)). And once a video becomes the default result for a search, it tends to stay there: the top lullaby videos are 5 to 16 years old and still add tens to hundreds of millions of views a year.

What parents type into YouTube shows what they actually want. In the US, the top suggestion after both "lullaby for babies" and "sleep music for babies" is **"no ads"**, followed by rain, "black screen", "live" and "24 hours" ([YouTube suggest](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&ds=yt&hl=en&gl=us&q=lullaby%20for%20babies); [YouTube suggest](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&ds=yt&hl=en&gl=us&q=sleep%20music%20for%20babies)). In India the first filter is **language**: suggestions after "lullaby for babies" are Hindi, Tamil, Malayalam, Kannada, Telugu and Marathi ([YouTube suggest](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&ds=yt&hl=en&gl=in&q=lullaby%20for%20babies)), and "baby sleep" brings up religious options such as dua and Quran ([YouTube suggest](https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&ds=yt&hl=en&gl=in&q=baby%20sleep)). These map neatly onto what a device can offer that YouTube cannot: no ads ever, a screen that goes dark, the family's own language, and a session that ends by itself. The one demand Cheeko should not copy is "24 hours", for the safety reasons in the next section.

## Children fall asleep in about 20 minutes, so bedtime audio should stop soon after

Lullaby audio today comes in four lengths: 20 to 40 second Shorts for discovery, 2 to 3.5 minute sung songs, 30 to 60 minute sung compilations, and 2 to 24-hour instrumental loops that run while the child sleeps. The research points Cheeko away from the longest of these.

Healthy children take about **20 minutes** to fall asleep. A meta-analysis of 79 studies gives a pooled time of **19.4 minutes** ([Adelaide](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/d9197850-c796-4201-8de5-8004247fb58d)), and in a study of 97 children aged 2 to 6, nearly all fell asleep within 48 minutes, older children faster ([Turku](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/34547242?lang=en_US)). What improves sleep is a **consistent nightly routine**: in a study of about 10,000 young children in many countries, the more consistent the routine, the shorter the time to fall asleep and the fewer the night wakings ([Mindell 2015](https://socsci3.tau.ac.il/clinic/articles/Mindell%202015%20-%20bedtime%20routines.pdf)). An older poll of 1,473 parents found a caregiver staying until the child fell asleep was among the factors most linked to poorer sleep ([Mindell 2009](https://www.nationaljewish.org/NJH/media/pdf/Meltzer%20References/Mindell-%282009%29-Developmental-Aspects-of-Sleep-Hygiene-Sleep-Med.pdf)). Nobody has tested whether a talking device that keeps a child company has the same effect, but it is a good reason not to make Cheeko's voice the thing a child needs in order to sleep.

Safety guidance also favours **short, timed play**. When researchers measured 14 infant sleep machines at full volume, every one was louder than the **50 dBA** hospital-nursery limit at 30 cm (dBA is sound level weighted the way ears hear it). They averaged **79 dBA at 30 cm, 71 dBA at 1 m and 63 dBA at 2 m**, and three passed 85 dBA, the adult workplace limit ([NEJM Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/sleep-machines-damage-infant-hearing-nejm-jw.NA34176)). The authors told parents to place machines more than 2 m away, use the lowest volume and run them briefly, and asked makers to cap volume and **add an auto-off timer** ([AAP release](https://www.enewspf.com/?p=101985)). The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2023 noise statement adds that children's small ear canals boost high frequencies and that this kind of hearing loss is usually permanent ([AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2023/american-academy-of-pediatrics-sounds-the-alarm-on-excessive-noise-and-risks-to-childrens-hearing-in-updated-policy-statement/)). Continuous noise as a sleep aid rests on "very low" quality evidence ([Riedy 2021](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1421191/article/noise-as-a-sleep-aid-a-systematic-review)), and only a third of white-noise studies in a 2022 review found better sleep ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34964434/)).

Light matters as much as sound. An hour of bright light before bed suppressed preschoolers' melatonin (the sleep hormone) by **87.6%**, and it stayed low 50 minutes later, although that test used 1,000 lux, far brighter than a small screen ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835497)). In preschoolers with sleep problems, even 1 to 10 lux in the last 35 minutes before bed was linked to about 6 extra minutes to fall asleep, and 75 lux or more to about 15 extra minutes ([UW ResearchWorks](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/handle/1773/42394)). The Indian Academy of Pediatrics advises no screen time under age 2 and says screens must never displace sleep ([Indian Pediatrics](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf)). Put together, a single lullaby should run about **2.5 to 4 minutes**, a bedtime session about **15 to 20 minutes** ending in a slow fade, and the screen should be dark for nearly all of it.

## Moon, home and the child's name: lyrics that soothe without scaring

Around the world, lullabies are about sleep, the mother (more often than the father), blessings and prayers, nature, and hopes for the child's future ([University of Ioannina](https://olympias2.lib.uoi.gr/items/69c614d2-613c-4474-a4b4-5b32ca2de02b); [Science Norway](https://partner.sciencenorway.no/history-music-sleep/lullabies-are-not-as-innocent-as-people-think/2507336)). Song is used to help children sleep in about 97% of cultures studied ([Science Norway](https://partner.sciencenorway.no/history-music-sleep/lullabies-are-not-as-innocent-as-people-think/2507336)). Traditional lullabies also carry a darker layer that a modern product should drop. Researcher Aubinet found everything from cheerful songs to laments, funeral scenes and the falling baby of Rock-a-bye, and notes that Western lullabies became gentler only in the 19th century ([Science Norway](https://partner.sciencenorway.no/history-music-sleep/lullabies-are-not-as-innocent-as-people-think/2507336)). A study of Lithuanian lullabies concluded that threatening ones were used to scare and discipline ([Vilnius University](https://journals.vu.lt/td/article/view/32742)), and an 18th-century Bengal lullaby carried fear of Maratha raiders ([Daily Star / ANN](https://asianews.network/?p=305355)). The useful move is to flip the night from scary to friendly: the moon keeps watch and everyone is safe at home.

### Indian lullabies share a moon-uncle, a sleep-aunt and a cradle

Every major Indian language has its own lullaby genre and a signature soothing sound. Hindi has lori, Tamil thalattu, Malayalam tharattu, Telugu jola pata, Marathi angai geet, Odia nanabaya gita and Bengali ghum-parani gaan ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lullaby)); Kannada has laali songs, with refrains such as "laali" in Telugu, "jo jo" in Kannada and "aararo" in Tamil ([The Better India](https://thebetterindia.com/parenting/indian-lullabies-sleep-babies-learning-culture-language-benefits-parenting-10879782)). Tamil thalattu are built on repeated refrains, and the singer stretches or shortens them to match how sleepy the child is ([ICOLLITE abstract](https://icollite.upi.edu/file/ppt/ABS-_ICOLLITE-25184-_The_Evolution_and_Socio-Cultural_Reflections_of_Tamil_Lullabies.pdf)). Some Punjabi loris have no special meaning and are valued simply for their sweet sound ([Punjab University](https://ocm.pu.edu.pk/website/journal/article/646312e5bdc4b/abstract)). Bengali mothers ask the "aunts of sleep" to bring sleep and ask Uncle Moon to bless the child's forehead ([Daily Star / ANN](https://asianews.network/?p=305355)). The famous Malayalam lullaby Omanathinkal Kidavo (1813) never uses the word for sleep at all; it soothes through a long chain of gentle comparisons of the child to the moon, flowers and pearls ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omanathinkal_Kidavo)). Values come through softly: in a Gujarati study of 14 halarda, moral values such as love, security and fearlessness appeared in 8 ([Central University of Gujarat](https://www.cugujarat.ac.in/AllDocuments/Department/ResearchProgramme/Abstract%20Data/Education/Dr.%20Minalba%20Jadeja/M.A/8_M.A._Devraj.pdf)). These shared motifs (Uncle Moon, sleep as a visiting aunt or fairy, the rocking cradle, birds flying home, the cow and calf, praise of the child) are ideas anyone can use with fresh words.

### Many "traditional" Hindi loris are copyrighted film songs

Many loris that Indian families think of as folk songs came from Hindi films of the 1940s to 1970s and are still under copyright. They include "So jaa rajkumari" from Zindagi (1940), described as the first Hindi film lori ([Atul's Song A Day](https://atulsongaday.me/2026/03/17/so-jaa-laadley/)); "Chanda Mama Door Ke" from Vachan (1955) ([Madhulika Liddle](https://madhulikaliddle.com/tag/balraj/)); "Chanda re" from Lajwanti (1958) ([Atul's Song A Day](https://atulsongaday.me/2009/04/08/chanda-re/)); "Nanhi kali sone chali" from Sujata (1959) ([Scroll](https://thereel.scroll.in/837628/scroll_in)); "Aa ri nindiya" from Rishte Naate (1965) ([India Forums](https://www.indiaforums.com/forum/post/163568552)); "Main gaaun tum so jaao" from Brahmachari (1968) ([Atul's Song A Day](https://atulsongaday.me/2009/01/08/main-gaaun-tum-so-jaao/)); and "Chanda hai tu" from Aradhana (1969) ([India Forums](https://www.indiaforums.com/forum/post/163553472)). Film loris also often hint at tragedy or separation, which is the opposite of what a bedtime device needs ([Atul's Song A Day](https://atulsongaday.me/2026/03/17/so-jaa-laadley/)). Whether the best-known "Lalla lori" is folk or film was not settled by the research, so that hook should be avoided too.

### Simple, repeated, soft-ending lines carry the soothing

Lullaby lines are short and repetitive, usually in a rocking triple or 6/8 time, with long pauses between sections and simple tonic-dominant harmony, and babies respond more to unaccompanied singing ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lullaby)). Adults could still recognise lullabies when the words were filtered out, so the sung delivery carries much of the effect ([Trehub 1993](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trehub.pdf)). Lyric-writing guides stress rhyme, the same number of syllables per line, and parallel structure, and name the moon as a classic symbol of comfort ([Local Learning Network](https://locallearningnetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Lally-Write-our-Own-Lullaby.pdf)). Songwriters advise few lines that end on hard consonants like k and t, and leaning on stretched vowels and n and ng sounds ([Writing Codidact](https://writing.codidact.com/q/4681)). The child's name works best once in a refrain line, held on a long note and never rushed ([macmyths](https://macmyths.com/how-to-make-a-personalized-lullaby-with-your-childs-name/)). Carnegie Hall's Lullaby Project has written original lullabies with more than 2,800 families in over 20 languages, starting from a parent's letter to the child and often using the child's name ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/zero2eight/carnegie-halls-lullaby-project-helps-parents-write-love-songs-to-their-infants/)). Neonatal music guidance says to switch from words to humming when a baby shows stress ([Draeger](https://www.draeger.com/Content/Documents/Content/NICU_Music_Final.pdf)), which suggests every lullaby should end by thinning into a hum.

Age changes the words more than the music. For babies and toddlers, sound matters more than meaning: vocables, the name and soft vowels. For preschoolers, rhyme helps learning: a review of 12 studies with 5,299 preschoolers linked nursery-rhyme experience and "finish the line" games to rhyme and sound awareness ([ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1097164.pdf)), and caregiver singing in infancy is linked with later vocabulary, though only as a correlation ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8450347)). No lullaby research covers 6 to 8 year olds, so a short calm story told in song is a reasonable guess. On language mixing, big Indian channels publish Hindi and English songs separately and no creator specialising in Hinglish lullabies was found ([Qoruz](https://qoruz.com/Hindi_Rhymes_For_Kids_HooplaKidz/youtube)). Because babies relax even to foreign-language lullabies ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2020/10/research-shows-lullabies-in-any-language-relax-babies)), mixing languages is safe as long as the melody and the refrain stay the same.

## Bells, one warm voice and careful AI: how lullabies are made in 2026

The commercial baby-sleep sound is soft mallets and bells. Rockabye Baby, the best-known lullaby label, describes its palette as glockenspiel, vibraphone, Mellotron, harps and bells, plus xylophones and celestas ([Rockabye Baby](https://rockabyebabymusic.com/blogs/rockabye-baby/interview-with-rockabye-baby-to-queen-producer-leo-flynn-ii)); in 2007 it ruled out grand piano, strings, flutes and acoustic guitar purely for brand reasons ([Austin Chronicle](https://austinchronicle.com/music/2007-08-03/510935)). Indian lori, by contrast, is a voice-first tradition of a mother humming or a grandmother singing while rocking a cradle ([The Better India](https://thebetterindia.com/parenting/indian-lullabies-sleep-babies-learning-culture-language-benefits-parenting-10879782)). No source documents bansuri or santoor as traditional lori accompaniment; their use in "Indian sleep music" is a modern stock and meditation convention. Big kids' studios run large in-house teams (Super Simple Songs has over 40 composers, musicians, animators and educators in Toronto), but none has published how it records lullaby vocals ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/super-simple-songs-20th-anniversary-youtube/)).

The voice research is clearer than the production lore. Singing meant for babies is slower, has more low-frequency warmth and **longer pauses between phrases** ([Trainor 1997](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/trainor_et_al_1997.pdf)), and it is smooth and joined-up rather than accented ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/05/26/1178496342/what-is-it-about-a-lullaby-that-helps-kids-fall-asleep)). Fathers make the same changes as mothers ([Trainor Lab](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/TrehubEtAl1997)). Spotify's own lullaby project recorded four versions of each new song: two languages, an instrumental and a hummed version ([Spotify](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2021-05-07/spotify-for-the-record-explores-the-art-and-science-of-lullabies/)).

On mastering, no standard sets a loudness target for lullabies. Spotify plays music at -14 LUFS by default (LUFS measures average loudness; more negative is quieter) with a -19 "Quiet" option ([Spotify](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)), and the audio engineering society AES recommended -16 to -20 LUFS with peaks no higher than -1 dBTP for streaming ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/aes-update-loudness-recommendations-for-audio-only-streaming)). Small speakers set hard limits: phone speakers lose most sound below about 500 Hz and above about 6 kHz ([SoundGirls](https://soundgirls.org/lost-in-translation/)), so producers make bass audible through its higher harmonics, keep low end in mono, and check mixes through a 400 Hz to 6 kHz filter at low volume ([Sonarworks](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/learn/make-your-mix-sound-great-on-small-speakers); [Waves](https://www.waves.com/tips-for-great-mixes-on-small-speakers)). Hours-long sleep videos are made by building one seamless segment, crossfading its joins and repeating it, for example a 30-minute loop played 20 times for 10 hours ([TunePocket](https://www.tunepocket.com/make-long-music-tracks/)).

AI music tools now make sung lullabies from custom lyrics, but their terms differ sharply, and several changed between August and October 2026.

The strongest position for Altio is therefore the one it has already started: melodies and accompaniment written in its own code (`lullaby.py`), lyrics written by people, and AI used at most for the voice.

## Recommendations: a blueprint for Cheeko's original lullabies

These recommendations turn the findings above into settings. Where the research gives a direction but no number (tempo, volume steps, reverb), the number below is a starting point to test, not a proven optimum.

### Length: 3-minute songs inside a 20-minute session

| Item | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| One lullaby | 2.5 to 4 minutes: refrain, verse, refrain, verse, refrain, then a hummed tail |
| Default bedtime session | 20 minutes, then silence (blueprint below) |
| Timer choices for parents | 10, 20 or 30 minutes. If parents ask for longer, a parent-only 45 to 60 minute option at the quietest level, instrumental only |
| Never by default | All-night or "24 hour" loops |
| Short clips | 30 to 60 second versions only for marketing and discovery, never as the sleep product |

| Time | What plays | Level and screen |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:30 | One calm spoken line with the name ("Goodnight, Riya. Time to rest.") over a soft drone | Screen dims to a dark, warm moon, fully off by 1:00 |
| 0:30 to 4:00 | Song 1 with words, the child's "own" song, the same every night, about 72 BPM | Base sleep level |
| 4:00 to 7:30 | Song 2 with words, about 66 BPM | About 1 dB quieter |
| 7:30 to 11:00 | Song 3 hummed, no words, about 60 BPM | About 1 dB quieter |
| 11:00 to 18:30 | Instrumental tail: harp and music box thinning to a drone, with small changes each pass | A further 2 to 3 dB quieter, step by step |
| 18:30 to 20:00 | 90-second fade to silence, then stop | No sound at the end, no beep, screen off |

Adjust by age. For **0 to 1**, use mostly hummed and wordless songs, the same 2 or 3 every night, 10 to 15 minutes, with the screen off from the start. For **1 to 3**, a few simple repeated words and 15 to 20 minutes. For **3 to 6**, an optional 5 to 8 minute soft story first, then 2 or 3 lullabies, 15 to 25 minutes in all; singing along is fine early, but the second half is listen-only. For **6 to 8**, a calm breathing or "small journey home" narration over a soft bed, ending in one lullaby, 20 to 30 minutes, with no cliffhangers.

### Music: 60 to 72 beats per minute, a rocking sway and no drums

| Setting | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Time signature | 3/4 or 6/8, a rocking sway |
| Tempo | About 60 to 72 BPM on the beat you would rock a baby to (design choice; no study proves a number). Current samples A (72) and C (66) sit at the top of this range, so use 60 to 66 for later songs in a session |
| Slowing | A gentle slow-down over the last phrase of each song, as sample A already does |
| Melody | Within one octave, mostly step by step, few leaps, every phrase falling at its end |
| Rests | Long pauses between phrases |
| Harmony | Simple, consonant, mostly home chord and "five" chord |
| Dynamics | Flat and soft, no build-ups, no big final chord |
| Repetition | The refrain at least three times; verses reuse the same sentence frame |

### Voice: one warm human singer, with hummed and instrumental versions

Hire a **warm Indian female singer** with a comfortable mid-to-low range to record the English and Hindi sung versions under a work-for-hire contract, so Altio owns the recordings outright. A male "Papa" voice is a fine second option. No singing synthesiser handles Hindi, and AI music tools either do not list Hindi or have unproven quality, so a human singer is both the clearest on rights and the most authentic lori sound. Make **four versions of every song**: English words, Hindi words, hummed, and instrumental, all on the same melody so the tune becomes the family's song in any language.

Keep TTS for spoken lines only. The current goodnight sample C uses Cheeko's `English_PlayfulGirl` voice; for sleep content, test the calm IDs (`English_CalmWoman`, `English_Graceful_Lady`) slowed down against it with parents. Cheeko's own voice can say the one-line opener so the child knows the routine has started, but avoid lines like "Cheeko is right here with you" that make the device the child's sleep companion; "Everyone is home, everyone is safe" does the same job without the dependency. For the name, a sung name spliced from TTS into a human vocal will sound wrong, so record the singer saying the 200 or so most common names in the same session, and fall back to a pet name ("little one", "munna", "munni") for the rest. A later feature worth building is a **parent-voice option**, letting a parent record their own hum or verse over Cheeko's instrumental, which copies the "song of kin" idea from neonatal music therapy.

### Lyrics in English, Hindi and Hinglish: themes, structure and two starter drafts

| Use these themes | Avoid these themes |
|---|---|
| Moon and stars keeping watch, birds and animals going home, the cow and calf, the cradle or jhoola rocking, rain and river slowing down, mother, father and grandparents near, a gentle blessing ("grow kind and strong"), tomorrow's small joys | Threats or bogeymen ("sleep or X will come"), falling or injury, death, a parent leaving, the singer's sadness, disasters, in-law troubles, moral warnings, religion by default (offer faith packs separately if wanted) |

**Structure for every song.** Refrain, verse, refrain, verse, refrain, then a hummed tail. Lines of 5 to 8 syllables for ages 0 to 5 and up to 10 for ages 6 to 8, with the same count in every line of a verse so the tune repeats exactly. Rhyme in pairs (AABB) or on lines 2 and 4 (ABCB). End lines on open vowels or soft sounds (aa, o, oo, ee, n, m, ng, l, and in Hindi -aa, -i, -o, -re, -na, -ri, -mein), not on t, k or p. Put the child's name once in the refrain on a held note, never at a rhyme position, so any name fits. Reuse the verse frame with one word swapped. In the last refrain, drop words step by step: full line, then name and vocable, then vocable only, then hum. Vocables can follow each language's tradition (Tamil-style "aaraaro", Telugu "laali", Kannada "jo jo", Hindi "aa-aa, oo-oo", "hmm") with completely fresh words around them. The word "sleep" is optional; imagery soothes on its own. Keep everything low-energy: no questions that need an answer, no counting games, no jokes that make a child laugh out loud, no "let's go".

The two drafts below were written for this report to show the shape. They are starting points only: a native Hindi speaker should polish the Hindi, and a musician should confirm that no line or tune is close to an existing song before anything ships.

### Production settings for a small mono speaker

| Setting | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Loudness | Sleep content at -20 to -23 LUFS integrated, playtime songs at about -16, peaks at or below -1 dBTP. `lullaby.py` already masters to -20 with a -2 dBTP ceiling |
| Smoothness | Keep short-term loudness within a few LU of the average; gentle, slow compression; nothing louder than the melody. Keep the music box pin click very quiet |
| Low end | High-pass at about 150 to 250 Hz, tuned to the measured speaker. The pure-sine `soft_bass()` will barely be heard on a toy speaker and only uses up amplifier headroom, so give bass and drone notes added harmonics or drop them from the device master |
| Top end | Gentle roll-off from about 6 to 8 kHz; de-ess any TTS. Children's ears boost high frequencies |
| Melody range | Keep music box, bansuri and voice mostly between 500 Hz and 3 kHz, where small speakers work best |
| Stereo | Make a separate **mono** device master and check the reverb still sounds right after summing; keep full stereo for phone and marketing versions |
| Fades | Fade-in of several seconds on every track; 6 to 7 seconds at song end is fine; 60 to 120 seconds at session end |
| Long play | Do not store hour-long files. Play intro, then a 3 to 5 minute loop N times, then outro, with a 2 to 8 second crossfade placed inside a sustained pad, never on a melody note. Because the music is made in code, render each pass with small seeded changes so it never repeats exactly |
| Instruments | Voice first. Then harp, music box or celesta, soft pad, tanpura drone, and bansuri used sparingly. One arrangement per song should be voice and drone only, the closest match to real lori singing |
| Check | Listen to every master at low volume on the actual Cheeko speaker, not only on studio monitors |

### Device and firmware settings

| Setting | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Sleep-mode volume cap | Measure the real Cheeko speaker with a sound level meter at 30 cm, 1 m and 2 m. Set the sleep-mode maximum so the level at the child's head stays under 50 dBA, with a default around 40 to 45 dBA. Cheeko is a toy a child may pull onto the pillow, so the 30 cm figure matters most. If a ceiling fan drowns it out, move Cheeko a little closer within the cap rather than raising the cap |
| Auto-off | On by default at 20 minutes; choices of 10, 20 and 30; no "never" option in the normal menu |
| Ending | Finish the current phrase, fade over 60 to 120 seconds, then stop silently. Do not auto-play other content afterwards. If a stream drops mid-song, fade out rather than cut, and prefer cached files for bedtime |
| Screen | Dim to the lowest warm setting within 30 to 60 seconds and switch off; no animation. For under-2 profiles, off from the start |
| Quiet state | No beeps, notifications or voice prompts while sleep mode is running |
| Starting it | As of the September 2026 firmware check, the voice agent cannot start playback, so ship lullabies as their own Radio station and, ideally, a dedicated sleep RFID card: one tap in a dark room, the same session every night |
| Parent guidance | Printed and in-app: place Cheeko across the room, about 2 m from the child's head, never inside a cot |
| Measure it | Log session start, end and the end reason (timer, parent stop, child stop) alongside the existing `content_track_start` events, so the team can see whether sessions run to the timer and which songs families keep |

### What to avoid

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| Any real song, melody or lyric, including public-domain ones like Twinkle Twinkle, Rock-a-bye, Hush Little Baby and Brahms' lullaby | Cheeko's standing rule is original-only, which is stricter than the law |
| Hindi film loris and their opening phrases: "Chanda Mama Door Ke", "So jaa rajkumari", "Chanda re", "Nanhi kali sone chali", "Aa ri nindiya", "Main gaaun tum so jaao", "Chanda hai tu"; also the "Lalla lori" hook | Copyrighted, and families recognise them instantly |
| Titles and hooks of popular kids'-channel lullabies such as "Nindiya Rani", "Munna Raja" or "Chandamama Raave" | Too close to well-known existing works |
| Prompting AI tools with artist names or song titles; using Suno outputs in any AI training; Udio; Lyria until its terms are confirmed; MiniMax-Music3 unless the interface label is acceptable | Licence terms forbid it or leave rights unclear |
| Threats, falling, death, a parent leaving, sadness, disasters, moral warnings | Traditional but frightening |
| Questions, sing-along or counting in the second half; exclamations; jokes; "let's go" | They wake children up |
| Drums, shakers, heartbeat sounds, sub-bass, bright highs, sudden chimes or clicks | Arousing or inaudible on a toy speaker |
| A new random lullaby every night | Familiar songs calm best; repeat the same 2 or 3 |
| Claims such as "puts your child to sleep", "asleep in 3 minutes", "matches your baby's heartbeat", "white noise sleep aid" or "screen-free" | Not supported by evidence, or simply untrue for Cheeko. Say "calming", "a bedtime routine" and "no ads" instead |

A sensible first release is three core songs in four versions each (English, Hindi, hummed, instrumental), one 3 to 5 minute instrumental loop for the session tail, and spoken opener lines with the name slot. Add one regional language at a time with a native-speaker lyricist; Tamil has the largest proven lullaby audience, and Malayalam shows the clearest gap between demand and supply.

## Conclusion

The YouTube charts teach a lesson that is easy to misread. Famous tunes win on YouTube because parents search for names they know, but search is not how a child meets Cheeko at bedtime; the device is already in the room. What the science says calms a child, a slow and simple song in one warm voice repeated night after night, is something an original song does just as well, and what parents keep asking YouTube for (no ads, a black screen, their own language, nothing that interrupts mid-song) is exactly what a dedicated device can promise and a video platform cannot. The biggest open space is calm, ad-free bedtime audio in Indian languages, where the most-viewed Hindi "lullaby" is a 70-year-old film song and long-form lori audio barely exists.

The real risks for Cheeko are not musical. They are accidentally echoing a beloved film lori, and playing too loud, too close, for too long. Both are solved with process and firmware rather than talent: an originality check on every melody and lyric, a measured volume cap, a timer and a dark screen. The evidence also leaves honest gaps. No study tests a lullaby tempo against sleep, no trial shows that recorded lullabies help healthy children fall asleep at home, and Cheeko's own speaker has not yet been measured. Measuring the speaker is the first job, and Cheeko's own session logs, collected with parents' consent, can then show which songs families keep and whether sessions run to the timer.
