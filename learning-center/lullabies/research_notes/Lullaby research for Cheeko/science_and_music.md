# The Science and Music of Lullabies: Features, Effects, Safe Volume and Duration for Children 0 to 8

Scope note: pattern research only. Nothing here should be used to copy any real song, melody or lyric. Sources are mostly abstracts, open-access full texts (PMC) and press releases from the research institutions. Where only press coverage or product blogs were available, that is flagged. Studies older than 15 years (pre-2011) are marked [OLDER].

## 1. Musical features of lullabies across cultures (tempo, meter, pitch, contour, repetition, harmony, dynamics, timbre)

### Takeaway
Across many cultures, lullabies are recognisable from their sound alone. They are slow, rhythmically and melodically simple, low in accent and energy, narrow in pitch range, repetitive, with falling melodic contours and a looser, less "driving" beat than dance or play songs. The research does not give hard numbers (BPM, keys, intervals). It describes direction ("slower", "smaller range"), not exact targets.

### Cited Findings
- Mehr, Singh, York, Glowacki & Krasnow (2018, *Current Biology* 28:356-368) used vocal music from 86 mostly small-scale societies. Listeners heard excerpts of lullabies, dance songs, healing songs and love songs and judged each one's function. Lullabies and dance songs were identified most accurately — [PubMed 29395919](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29395919/); [Neuroscience News / Harvard summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/music-universal-language-8370/)
- In that study, lullabies were heard as slow, sparse, rhythmically and melodically simple, more melancholy and less energetic. Dance songs were the opposite: faster, more complex, "happier" and "more exciting". Note: this profile comes from author comments to the press — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/music-universal-language-8370/)
- In the 2018 study, 1,000 internet listeners **in the United States and India** rated melodic complexity, rhythmic complexity, tempo, steady beat, arousal, valence and pleasantness. Musical features explained unique variance in function judgments beyond context cues such as singer gender — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/music-universal-language-8370/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29395919/)
- Mehr et al. (2019, *Science*, "Universality and diversity in human song", Natural History of Song corpus): rhythmic complexity separated dance songs (more complex) and lullabies (less complex) from other song types. The authors frame universals as "clusters of correlated behaviors, such as slow soothing lullabies" — [HRAF hypothesis summary](https://hraf.yale.edu/documents/1260/hypotheses/4295); [PMC7001657](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/7001657)
- Bainbridge et al. (2021) summarise the Natural History of Song acoustics: lullabies are "typically less accented, slower in tempo, have smaller pitch ranges, and more variable meter than other songs". Adult raters judged them lower in melodic and rhythmic complexity, slower, with a **less steady beat**, and lower in arousal — [Bainbridge et al., PMC8220405](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405)
- Trehub, Unyk & Trainor (1993) [OLDER]: lullaby/adult-song pairs from world field recordings were matched for tempo, singing style and orchestration. Western adults still picked out the lullaby above chance, so tempo alone does not define a lullaby. Proposed cues: "simplicity, repetitiveness, and falling pitch contours" — [Trehub 1993 PDF](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trehub.pdf)
- Trehub et al. (1993/1997) [OLDER]: when parents sang to their infant, the song was **higher in pitch and slower in tempo** than the same song sung with no infant present. In one experiment, **Indian-descent mothers sang Hindi songs** and both Indian and North American listeners identified the infant-directed versions above chance — [Trehub 1993 PDF](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/1993_Trehub.pdf); [Trehub et al. 1997 record](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/TrehubEtAl1997)
- Trainor (1996) [OLDER]: mothers singing to 4- to 7-month-olds made play songs relatively **more rhythmic** and lullabies relatively less so. This suggests the two genres carry different messages (arouse vs soothe) — [Trainor 1996 PDF](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/trainor_1996.pdf)
- Hilton, Moser et al. (2022, *Nature Human Behaviour* 6:1545-1556): 1,615 recordings from 21 societies. Infant-directed song showed "consistent changes in tempo and timbre" against adult-directed song, while median pitch "remained largely the same during song". The large pitch rise belongs to infant-directed **speech**, not song — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2022/07/people-around-world-tend-to-talk-to-babies-in-higher-voice); [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/acoustic-regularities-in-infant-directed-speech-and-song-across-c/)
- Press coverage of Hilton et al. lists "purer timbre" among the infant-directed features. This summary covers speech and song together, so it is not song-specific — [IFLScience](https://iflscience.com/baby-talk-seems-to-be-a-universal-language-across-the-globe-64580)
- A qualitative Thai ethnomusicology study (72 lullabies, 20 informants) reports tempi of about 50-55 BPM, "slow and flexible". This is one locality only, so treat it as weak evidence — [J. Studies in Culture (Thai)](https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JSC/article/view/278837)
- Trehub notes a cultural split: Western caregivers tend to sing lively play songs face to face, while many non-Western caregivers sing soothing lullabies to infants they are carrying — [Trehub, "Music lessons from infants"](https://researchgate.net/profile/Sandra-Trehub/publication/286965246_Music_lessons_from_infants/links/568fd2d308aee91f69a12fdc/Music-lessons-from-infants.pdf)

### Inferences
- Design levers supported by the evidence for ORIGINAL Cheeko lullabies: slow tempo; few notes and a narrow range (roughly within an octave, ideally less); stepwise motion; phrases that fall at their ends; lots of repetition (repeated lines and refrains); soft, unaccented attacks; a loose rubato pulse rather than a hard metronomic beat; a solo warm voice with sparse or no accompaniment; low arousal throughout, with no build-ups or drops.
- "Less steady beat" and "more variable meter" in the corpus data argue against a quantised, drum-led backing track. A gentle swaying triple feel (3/4 or 6/8) fits the "rocking" description, but no study tested 3/4 vs 4/4 directly (see Gaps).
- Because Hilton found song pitch is *not* raised for infants (unlike speech), Cheeko does not need a high, chirpy voice for lullabies. A warm mid-to-low register is consistent with the evidence. The "higher pitch" result from Trehub 1997 concerns parents singing the same song to a baby, and it is older and Western-heavy.
- The Indian-listener and Hindi-song data (Mehr 2018 raters, Trehub 1993) support the idea that these cues also work for Indian families, not only Western ones.

### Gaps
- No study found gives a validated BPM range, preferred key/mode (major vs minor), interval sizes or harmonic rules for lullabies. The full Mehr 2019 supplementary data may hold per-song tempo values, but I could not read them this session.
- No experimental comparison of triple meter (3/4, 6/8) vs duple meter for soothing was found.
- The Hilton 2022 full text (song-specific direction of each acoustic feature) returned HTTP 403 and could not be read. "Purer timbre" and "tempo changes" for song should be checked against the paper.
- No peer-reviewed acoustic analysis of Indian lullabies (lori) was found in this pass.

## 2. Evidence on effects: heart rate, arousal, crying, sleep onset, NICU, live vs recorded, familiar vs unfamiliar

### Takeaway
Good evidence shows that lullabies and infant-directed singing **lower arousal in the short term**: lower heart rate, smaller pupils, damped skin conductance, delayed or reduced distress. This holds even for unfamiliar foreign-language lullabies. Singing beats speech for calming. Familiar songs give the biggest mood lift. The NICU evidence favours live, adapted music over recordings. Direct evidence that recorded lullabies shorten sleep onset in healthy home settings is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **Bainbridge et al. (2021, *Nature Human Behaviour* 5:256-264):** 144 US infants aged 2.1-14.3 months (mean 7.2) heard 16 solo-voice, unaccompanied songs from 15 societies (8 lullabies vs 8 non-lullabies), each trial 14 s long. Heart rate fell during lullabies (d = 0.36) and did not change during non-lullabies, and the difference was significant (p = 0.007). Pupils were smaller during lullabies (n = 30). Lullabies attenuated the rise in electrodermal arousal across the session. The effect did not vary with age (2-14 months). Parents chose the lullaby as the song they would use to soothe in nearly all comparisons — [PMC8220405](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405)
- Bainbridge limitations: one Western society only; the authors cannot fully rule out that infants relaxed because the songs resembled lullabies they had heard before; which acoustic features cause relaxation is still open; most babies paid attention for about 5 minutes per session — [PMC8220405](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2020/10/research-shows-lullabies-in-any-language-relax-babies)
- **Loewy et al. (2013, *Pediatrics*):** 272 premature infants (≥32 weeks; respiratory distress, sepsis and/or small for gestational age) across 11 NICUs, each infant serving as its own control. Three live interventions were given by music therapists 3x/week for 2 weeks: a parent-preferred lullaby ("song of kin"), the ocean disc (whooshing sound) and the gato box (heartbeat-like drum). Heart rate was significantly lower during the lullaby and rhythm interventions. Parent-preferred lullabies were linked to more sucking and higher caloric intake. Parental stress fell — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/28710-music-therapy-premature-babies-health.html); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/pregnancy/music-aids-preemie-physiologic-developmental-function-675337.html); [Pathways.org](https://pathways.org/research-effectiveness-of-music-therapy-to-maintain-improve-premature-infants-health)
- Loewy 2013, press reports: the ocean disc gave the best sleep and the drum gave the largest gain in sucking. The authors argue recorded music can **overstimulate** NICU babies, while live music can be adapted to the infant ("Live sounds are the key") — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/28710-music-therapy-premature-babies-health.html); [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/567094/tiny-preemies-get-a-boost-from-live-music-therapy/)
- **Cirelli & Trehub (2020, *Developmental Psychology* 56:861-868):** 136 infants aged 8 and 10 months were tested with the Still-Face procedure. Parents tried to soothe with a familiar song, an unfamiliar song or expressive talking. Talking *increased* negative affect. Both kinds of song reduced negative affect and increased gaze to the parent. Familiar songs gave the largest benefit plus more smiling. In a subset, skin conductance was highest for speech, lowest for unfamiliar songs and in between for familiar songs, so familiar songs may soothe while also exciting — [UCSF Sound Health](https://soundhealth.ucsf.edu/node/8001); [UTM news](https://www.utm.utoronto.ca/node/4951)
- Corbeil, Trehub & Peretz (2015, *Infancy*): infants listened to singing about **twice as long** as to speech before reaching distress — [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/singing-calms-baby-longer-than-talking); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/singling-calms-infants-2950/amp/)
- Preschool nap study (University of Oregon, small, not randomised): children took about 34 min to fall asleep in silence vs about 22 min with recorded lullabies (p = .04), from only 13 observations. **Weak evidence** — [U. Oregon](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~rmoore/res-prenap.htm)
- Harmat et al. (2008, *J Adv Nurs*) [OLDER, adults]: 94 students with sleep complaints were randomised to 45 min of relaxing classical music, an audiobook or nothing at bedtime for 3 weeks. Music improved sleep quality (PSQI). The audiobook and control groups did not improve — [Semmelweis summary](https://semmelweis.hu/psychophysiology/2015/11/18/harmat-l-takacs-j-bodizs-r-music-improves-sleep-quality-in-students-j-adv-nurs-62-3-327-335-2008/)
- A feasibility study of the Moshi audio sleep app notes that "the impact of music therapy or storytelling on sleep outcomes in young children has not been reported". Rigorous child trials of bedtime audio are missing — [Out Like a Light, PMC9368592](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9368592/)

### Inferences
- The strongest claim Cheeko can honestly make is "calming" or "wind-down", **not** "puts your child to sleep faster". Sleep-onset evidence for recorded music in healthy children is weak.
- Familiarity helps: repeating the *same* small set of Cheeko lullabies every night should, over time, gain the "familiar song" advantage (Cirelli & Trehub). Constant novelty works against this.
- Live, responsive singing beats recordings in the NICU. A device cannot be live, but it can imitate some "live" qualities: a natural human voice (not robotic), slight tempo flexibility, gradual slowing, and a parent option such as recording the parent's own voice or inserting the child's name.
- Singing calms better than talking (Cirelli; Corbeil). For the youngest children, a sung lullaby should come before or replace a spoken story.

### Gaps
- No trial found tests recorded (device-played) lullabies against silence for sleep onset or night wakings in healthy home infants or toddlers.
- The full Loewy 2013 abstract could not be retrieved from PubMed (a cookie wall), so effect sizes and details such as oxygen saturation results are from press coverage only.
- No study found isolates which single feature (tempo, contour, timbre, range) drives the calming effect. The Bainbridge group says this work is in progress.

## 3. Heartbeat-like rhythms, rocking motion, and the "60 to 80 BPM" idea

### Takeaway
Rocking and carrying at a steady walking pace measurably calm infants (heart rate drops within about 30 s), and heartbeat-like drumming lowered heart rate in preemies. However, **no primary source was found that establishes 60-80 BPM as an ideal lullaby tempo**. The figure appears to be folklore or background claims, and it is matched to adult (or maternal) resting heart rate, not the infant's.

### Cited Findings
- **Ohmura, Kuroda et al. (2022, *Current Biology*):** 21 infants from newborn to 7 months. Being carried by a walking mother calmed crying infants and slowed their heart rate **within 30 seconds**. A rocking cot had a similar effect. Being held by a seated mother or lying in a still crib did not. Putting the baby down before about 5 minutes raised heart rate again. Suggested protocol: about 5 min of carrying, then 5-8 min of sitting while holding, before laying the baby down. This was a proof of concept with a small sample — [RIKEN press release](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2022/20220914_1/index.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/09/220913110154.htm); [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/othersciences/psychology/infant-walking-11194.html)
- Loewy 2013: a live, entrained heartbeat-like rhythm (gato box) lowered heart rate in premature infants, alongside the lullaby — [Live Science](https://livescience.com/28710-music-therapy-premature-babies-health.html)
- The 60-80 BPM figure was found only as background in a TU Dresden conference poster, which itself treats "whether lullabies are performed within a certain tempo range" as an open question — [TU Dresden poster](https://tu-dresden.de/ing/elektrotechnik/ias/socialtouch/ressourcen/dateien/Poster-IASAT25-Benjamin-2.pdf)
- A German university project is collecting lullabies to test whether lullaby tempo matches the speed of caregiver stroking across cultures. No results yet — [UniBw München project page](https://unibw.de/hum-psychologie/online-liste-forschungsprojekte/lullaby_english)
- Thai lullaby fieldwork reports about 50-55 BPM (qualitative, one locality) — [J. Studies in Culture](https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JSC/article/view/278837)

### Inferences
- A reasonable working range for Cheeko's original lullabies is **about 55-75 BPM** (counted at the dotted-quarter or "sway" level in 6/8). It sits in the "slow" band the corpus studies describe and near a calm adult or maternal pulse and a slow walking or rocking sway. Label it a **design choice, not an evidence-based optimum**.
- Infant resting heart rate is well above 60-80 BPM (newborns are typically over 100 BPM; general physiology knowledge, not sourced this session). So any marketing line saying the music "matches your baby's heartbeat" would be inaccurate. "Matches a calm, rocking rhythm" is safer.
- Ohmura's "5 minutes of motion, then a further 5-8 minutes of calm" suggests a soothing audio block of at least 10-15 minutes, so the child stays settled through the hand-off to bed.

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing lullaby tempi (e.g. 50 vs 70 vs 90 BPM) on infant or toddler physiology or sleep onset was found.
- The optimal rocking frequency (Hz) for infants was not covered by recent primary sources found in this pass.

## 4. White noise, pink noise and nature sounds: evidence and safety (dB levels, distance, duration)

### Takeaway
Evidence that continuous noise improves sleep is **very low to low quality and mixed**. The safety concern is real: commercial infant sleep machines at full volume far exceed the 50 dBA hospital-nursery benchmark at the crib, and some exceed adult workplace limits. Guidance converges on these rules: keep the device far from the child (about 2 m / 7 ft is the best-supported figure), use the lowest effective volume (under about 50 dBA at the child's ear), and limit run time (timer or switch-off after sleep). No evidence was found that pink noise or nature sounds are better for children.

### Cited Findings
- **Hugh et al. (2014, *Pediatrics* 133:677-681, doi 10.1542/peds.2013-3617):** 14 infant sleep machines (ISMs) at full volume, measured with a simulated infant ear canal. **All** exceeded 50 dBA (the recommended hospital-nursery limit, averaged over 1 h) at 30 cm. Mean maximum output was 79 dBA at 30 cm, 71 dBA at 100 cm and 63 dBA at 200 cm. All but one exceeded 50 dBA at all three distances. Three exceeded 85 dBA at 30 cm, above adult workplace limits. At those levels for more than 8 hours, they exceed adult occupational limits for accumulated noise — [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1649352); [NEJM Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/sleep-machines-damage-infant-hearing-nejm-jw.NA34176); [Safety+Health](https://safetyandhealthmagazine.com/articles/10032-white-noise-from-sleep-machines-can-harm-babies-ears-study-finds)
- Hugh et al. recommendations: place machines **farther than 200 cm** from the crib, use the lowest volume, run them briefly. They also ask manufacturers to cap maximum output, add hearing-loss warnings and **include an auto-off timer**. Caveat: the study measured maximum output, not real-world use, and did not show actual hearing damage — [eNews Park Forest (AAP release)](https://www.enewspf.com/?p=101985); [NEJM Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/sleep-machines-damage-infant-hearing-nejm-jw.NA34176)
- **AAP policy statement (2023), "Preventing Excessive Noise Exposure in Infants, Children, and Adolescents"**: pediatricians should counsel families using infant sleep or noise machines on safe use. It notes children's smaller ear canals intensify higher-frequency sound, that duration matters as well as loudness, and that sensorineural hearing loss is usually irreversible. Coverage summarises it as: keep the machine far away, at the lowest volume, for a short time — [AAP news release](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2023/american-academy-of-pediatrics-sounds-the-alarm-on-excessive-noise-and-risks-to-childrens-hearing-in-updated-policy-statement/); [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org:443/English/news/Pages/sounds-the-alarm-on-excessive-noise-and-risks-to-children.aspx); [Hearing Review](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-loss/patient-care/pediatric-care/american-academy-of-pediatrics-updates-policy-statement-on-hearing-risks-for-children)
- **Conflict on the AAP and 50 dB:** several hospital and parenting sources attribute a "below 50 dB" AAP limit for the sleep environment. Care.com reports the AAP does not prescribe specific dB levels for infant white-noise machines. The 50 dBA figure originates as a **hospital nursery** benchmark — [Children's National](https://riseandshine.childrensnational.org/baby-sound-machines-and-hearing-loss/); [Care.com](https://www.care.com/c/?p=204325); [Hugh et al. via Journal Watch](https://clinician.nejm.org/nejm-jw.NA34176)
- **Conflict on distance:** Hugh/AAP release: >200 cm. Henry Ford Health: at least 7 ft. Yogasleep (manufacturer): about 6.5 ft. Children's National: at least 2 ft (the outlier) — [Henry Ford Health](https://www.henryford.com/blog/2026/06/are-white-noise-machines-safe-for-your-baby); [Children's National](https://riseandshine.childrensnational.org/baby-sound-machines-and-hearing-loss/); [Yogasleep (commercial)](https://yogasleep.com/blogs/give-sleep-a-chance-blog/how-loud-should-white-noise-be-for-baby)
- Practical points from clinician blogs: do not put the device in the crib, on the rail or near the head. Check the level at the mattress with a phone sound-meter app. Turn it off once the baby is asleep (Children's National). A physician quoted by Henry Ford says "A continuous, unchanging sound is often best for babies" — [Henry Ford Health](https://www.henryford.com/blog/2026/06/are-white-noise-machines-safe-for-your-baby); [Children's National](https://riseandshine.childrensnational.org/baby-sound-machines-and-hearing-loss/); [Babylist](https://babylist.com/hello-baby/sound-machine-safety-basics)
- **Riedy, Smith, Rocha & Basner (2021, *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 55:101385):** systematic review of 38 articles on continuous white or broadband noise and sleep. The quality of evidence that continuous noise improves sleep was "very low" (GRADE). Results ranged from improvement to disruption. The authors call for better studies before promoting noise as a sleep aid — [EM-Consulte abstract](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1421191/article/noise-as-a-sleep-aid-a-systematic-review); [HMP Global Q&A](https://www.hmpgloballearningnetwork.com/site/neurology/qas/systematic-review-cautions-promoting-white-noise-machines-viable-sleep-aid)
- A 2022 *J Clin Sleep Med* review of auditory stimulation (34 studies, 1,103 people): only 33% of white-noise studies reported improved sleep. There was no strong evidence for auditory stimulation, but no short-term adverse effects were reported — [PubMed 34964434](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34964434/)

### Inferences
- For Cheeko (a small speaker that may sit on a bedside table): build in a **hard volume cap for night or lullaby mode**, so that at a typical bedside distance the level at the child's head stays **under about 50 dBA**. Ideally aim for whisper-to-quiet-room levels (about 35-45 dBA). Verify with a calibrated meter at 30 cm, 1 m and 2 m, mirroring Hugh 2014.
- Put the default **auto-off timer** in firmware (Hugh's recommendation to manufacturers) and fade out rather than cutting off abruptly.
- Product copy for parents should say: place Cheeko across the room, not in or on the crib or bed, and away from the child's head (best-supported figure is about 2 m).
- A small speaker's limited bass means lullabies should be mixed for clarity at low volume. Avoid harsh high frequencies, because children's ear canals amplify them (AAP 2023).
- Do not market a "white noise" or "rain sounds" all-night mode as a sleep aid. The evidence is very low quality, and all-night exposure is the scenario the AAP and Hugh caution about.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence specific to **pink noise** or **nature sounds** (rain, ocean) for infant or child sleep was found in this pass.
- The commonly cited Spencer et al. (1990, *Arch Dis Child*) neonatal white-noise study was not verified this session [OLDER].
- The AAP 2023 policy statement full text (which may contain exact numbers) could not be opened. Indian Academy of Pediatrics guidance on sound machines or bedtime noise was not found.

## 5. Length: how long should bedtime audio play? Sleep-onset times, continuous vs fixed wind-down, screens and audio-only

### Takeaway
Healthy children typically take **about 20 minutes** to fall asleep (pooled average 19.4 min). Most preschoolers fall within about 0-48 min. A fixed, predictable bedtime routine shortens sleep onset and reduces night waking. Clinical guidance on sound points toward **short, timed** audio rather than all-night play, and toward switching off after sleep. Screens and bright light before bed delay sleep: preschoolers are very sensitive to evening light (about 88% melatonin suppression at 1,000 lux). Audio-only, dark-screen bedtime content is the low-risk format.

### Cited Findings
- **Sleep-onset norms:** an actigraphy meta-analysis (Galland et al., 2018, *Sleep*; 79 studies, ages 0-18) gives a pooled sleep-onset latency of **19.4 min**. A preschool actigraphy study (97 children aged 2-6) gives a 2.5th-97.5th percentile range of **0.2-48.4 min**, shorter in older children. Polysomnography gave about 25 min (preschool) and about 23 min (school-age) in a clinical sample — [Adelaide record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/d9197850-c796-4201-8de5-8004247fb58d); [Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/34547242?lang=en_US); [PMC4785891](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4785891)
- **Bedtime routines (Mindell et al. 2015, about 10,000 children, multinational):** a consistent nightly routine was linked to shorter sleep onset, earlier bedtimes and fewer night wakings, in a dose-dependent way (more consistent means better; starting earlier helps more) — [Mindell 2015 PDF](https://socsci3.tau.ac.il/clinic/articles/Mindell%202015%20-%20bedtime%20routines.pdf); [Sleep Review](https://sleepreviewmag.com/uncategorized/children-sleep-better-nightly-bedtime-routine/)
- **Mindell et al. 2009** [OLDER]: in a poll of 1,473 parents of children up to age 10, later bedtimes went with longer sleep onset. A caregiver staying until the child fell asleep was among the factors most strongly linked to poorer sleep. (Disclosure: Mindell consulted for Johnson & Johnson) — [Mindell 2009 PDF](https://www.nationaljewish.org/NJH/media/pdf/Meltzer%20References/Mindell-(2009)-Developmental-Aspects-of-Sleep-Hygiene-Sleep-Med.pdf)
- **Hugh 2014 / AAP:** use sound devices briefly, with an **auto-off timer**. Children's National: turn the machine off once the baby is asleep — [eNews Park Forest](https://www.enewspf.com/?p=101985); [Children's National](https://riseandshine.childrensnational.org/baby-sound-machines-and-hearing-loss/)
- **Ohmura 2022:** about 5 min of soothing motion plus 5-8 min of settled holding before the transfer to bed — [RIKEN](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2022/20220914_1/index.html)
- **Harmat 2008** (adults): 45 min of music at bedtime improved sleep quality — [Semmelweis](https://semmelweis.hu/psychophysiology/2015/11/18/harmat-l-takacs-j-bodizs-r-music-improves-sleep-quality-in-students-j-adv-nurs-62-3-327-335-2008/)
- **Light and screens:** Akacem, Wright & LeBourgeois (2018, *Physiological Reports*) exposed 10 preschoolers (mean about 4.3 y) to 1,000 lux for 1 h before bedtime. Melatonin was suppressed **87.6%** and stayed low 50 min after the light went off. In 7/10 children it had not recovered to 50% by then. Caveat: 1,000 lux is far brighter than a handheld screen — [PMC5835497](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835497); [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/today/node/27600)
- A UW thesis studied preschoolers who screened positive for sleep problems. Only light in the final 35 min before bed predicted sleep-onset latency: 1-10 lux was linked to about 6 extra minutes and ≥75 lux to about 15 extra minutes, compared with under 1 lux — [UW ResearchWorks](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/handle/1773/42394)
- **AAP (secondary sources):** screens off at least 1 hour before bed and devices out of the bedroom overnight. Some sources quote 30 minutes, so the figure conflicts — [Apple family guide citing AAP](https://support.apple.com/guide/aap-apple/bcbd8aaf4040/web); [Qustodio](https://pre.qustodio.com/en/blog/expert-back-to-school-advice/); [Atlanta Magazine](https://www.atlantamagazine.com/health/kids-and-screen-time/)
- **Indian Academy of Pediatrics (2022, *Indian Pediatrics* 59:235-244):** no screens under 2 years (except occasional video calls). At most 1 h/day of supervised screen time for 24-59 months. Under 2 h/day for 5-10 years. Screen time must not displace sleep. No specific "before bed" cutoff appeared in the material found — [Indian Pediatrics PDF](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf); [WHO IMSEAR record](https://imsear.searo.who.int/items/83e152e9-46a9-44a1-b8e7-8d3fe2e8cb22)
- **Recommended total sleep (AASM 2016, AAP-endorsed):** 4-12 months: 12-16 h/24 h including naps. 1-2 y: 11-14 h. 3-5 y: 10-13 h. 6-12 y: 9-12 h. No range is given under 4 months — [AASM methods PDF](https://aasm.org/resources/pdf/pediatricsleepdurationmethods.pdf); [Sleep Review](https://sleepreviewmag.com/sleep-health/demographics/age/aasm-releases-first-official-pediatric-sleep-duration-recommendation/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended Cheeko default: a fixed wind-down of about 15-20 minutes, then a 1-2 minute fade to silence, with parent-selectable 10 / 20 / 30 min options. No all-night loop by default.** The basis: typical sleep onset is about 20 min; Ohmura suggests 10-13 min of settling; Hugh and the AAP favour short, timed use; and a fixed routine is what drives the benefit (Mindell).
- Structure the session as a gradual descent: the energy, tempo and volume of each track step down slightly from the first lullaby to the last, ending in a long, very quiet repeating outro before the fade.
- The routine element matters more than the specific content: the same start cue, the same order, the same closing phrase every night.
- Avoid audio that "needs" the child to be awake (questions, calls to sing along) in the final minutes. Mindell 2009 found a caregiver staying until sleep was linked to poorer sleep, so audio meant to keep a child company until asleep could become a sleep-onset dependency (inference; not tested for audio).
- **Screen behaviour in bedtime mode:** Cheeko has a small screen. In lullaby mode the display should go dark, or very dim and warm, within the first minute. Even modest light in the last 35 min before bed can lengthen sleep onset. Make lullaby mode audio-only in practice. (Per house style, do not call the product "screen-free".)

### Gaps
- No trial compares a fixed-length wind-down with all-night audio in children for sleep onset, night waking or total sleep.
- No evidence was found on whether audio stopping mid-night (at a sleep-cycle arousal) wakes children. This is plausible but unstudied in this pass.
- No IAP-specific guidance on bedtime audio or devices in the bedroom was found.

## 6. Differences by age: 0-1, 1-3, 3-6, 6-8 and what bedtime audio suits each

### Takeaway
Most physiological evidence covers infants (0-14 months), where sung lullabies (not speech) work best and calming effects are consistent across the first year. For toddlers and preschoolers the evidence moves to routines, light and small, weak music studies. For 6-8-year-olds there is almost no child-specific evidence on bedtime audio, so guidance is extrapolated from routines and adult music studies.

### Cited Findings
- **0-1 year:** the lullaby calming effect (heart rate, pupils, arousal) did not differ between 2 and 14 months — [PMC8220405](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220405). Singing beats talking at 8-10 months, and familiar songs are best — [UCSF Sound Health](https://soundhealth.ucsf.edu/node/8001). Motion (carrying or rocking) calms 0-7-month-olds within 30 s — [RIKEN](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2022/20220914_1/index.html). Mothers make lullabies less rhythmic than play songs for 4-7-month-olds — [Trainor 1996](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/trainor_1996.pdf). Infant ears are most vulnerable to sound-machine output — [AAP 2023](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2023/american-academy-of-pediatrics-sounds-the-alarm-on-excessive-noise-and-risks-to-childrens-hearing-in-updated-policy-statement/)
- **Under 2:** IAP advises no screen exposure at all — [Indian Pediatrics 2022](https://www.indianpediatrics.net/mar2022/235.pdf)
- **3-6 years:** preschoolers are highly sensitive to evening light (about 88% melatonin suppression); the authors attribute this to clearer lenses and larger pupils — [PMC5835497](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835497); [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/today/node/27600). One small study found preschoolers fell asleep faster with recorded lullabies than in silence (22 vs 34 min) — [U. Oregon](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~rmoore/res-prenap.htm). A naptime dissertation with 2- and 4-year-olds compared silence, story reading, a cappella singing and guitar, but its results were not retrieved — [Biblioteca de la Guitarra listing](https://bibliotecadelaguitarra.com/es/articulo/10744/the-effect-of-silence-story-reading-acapella-singing-and-guitar-playing-on-inducing-sleep-in-preschool-children.html). Sleep onset gets shorter with age within 2-6 years — [Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/34547242?lang=en_US)
- **Routines across ages:** the benefit of a consistent bedtime routine was seen across the multinational sample of young children, with bedtime stories among typical routine elements — [Mindell 2015](https://socsci3.tau.ac.il/clinic/articles/Mindell%202015%20-%20bedtime%20routines.pdf); [Sleep Review / AASM](https://sleepreviewmag.com/uncategorized/children-sleep-better-nightly-bedtime-routine/)
- **6-8 years:** no child-specific bedtime-audio trial was found. The closest is an adult RCT where music (not an audiobook) improved sleep quality — [Semmelweis/Harmat 2008](https://semmelweis.hu/psychophysiology/2015/11/18/harmat-l-takacs-j-bodizs-r-music-improves-sleep-quality-in-students-j-adv-nurs-62-3-327-335-2008/). Story vs music in children remains unstudied — [PMC9368592](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9368592/)

### Inferences (proposed age profiles for Cheeko; design judgement, not tested)
- **0-1 (babies):** voice-led lullabies only, no story. Very simple, highly repetitive, narrow range, a slow swaying feel, a soft solo voice with minimal accompaniment. Lowest volume cap. The device is for the parent to use (placed about 2 m away). The same 2-3 lullabies every night to build familiarity. Wind-down about 10-15 min, then fade. Screen off.
- **1-3 (toddlers):** lullabies with a few simple, repeated words (child's name, "sleep", "moon", "stars"). An optional very short calm opening (1-2 min, e.g. "time to say goodnight to...") before the songs. No questions or calls to sing along in the second half. About 15-20 min.
- **3-6 (preschool):** a short soft story (about 5-8 min) followed by 2-3 lullabies (about 8-10 min), then fade. Total about 15-25 min. Sing-along is fine at the *start* only; the last phase is listen-only. Screen dark because of high light sensitivity.
- **6-8 (early school):** longer calm stories or gentle "wind-down" narration (a body-relaxation or breathing script) with a soft instrumental or hummed bed underneath, then a closing lullaby. 20-30 min option. Avoid exciting plots or cliffhangers near the end (inference).
- For all ages: the ending signature (a closing phrase plus a fade of 1-2 min) stays the same every night, as a sleep cue.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on bedtime audio specifically for 1-3-year-olds, or comparing lullaby vs story vs sing-along in any age group.
- No age-specific evidence on optimal bedtime-audio volume beyond infant sound-machine data.
- No Indian-specific data on bedtime routines or co-sleeping contexts (relevant because a bedside device may be close to a shared bed) was found in this pass.
